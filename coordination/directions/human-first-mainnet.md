This direction sits on top of [D7](self-service-borrower-app.md) (the app, the stability proof, the screen, gates 1 to 6) and does not replace its gates; it adds the two operator requirements, the research the second requires, and the list of what else must be true before the checkpoint.

## Measurable outcome, evidence, simpler route, stop condition

- **Human outcome or decision:** the operator's mainnet decision rests on a packet in which a crypto-naive person can borrow, use and repay dollars with the pool holding USDC, at a measured all-in cost, under a named lender of record. The human scorecard stays `M = B / sponsor money consumed`.
- **Evidence sought:** (a) a measured cash-in and cash-out round trip for a 10 USDC loan and a 0.25 USDC interest payment, with every fee; (b) a trust interview that produces an explainable scorecard with measured cost per assessment and shadow-scored outcomes on testnet; (c) counsel's answer on lender of record and jurisdiction; (d) D7 gates 2 and 3 passed.
- **Competing simpler routes:** direct cash to the same people; a licensed partner lending from its balance sheet with the pool as ledger (D8); an interest deducted at disbursement so no interest payment rail is needed at all (option D below). Each is compared in the packet.
- **Stop or change condition:** no lawful lender of record in the chosen jurisdiction; the measured round-trip rail cost above 10 percent of a 10 USDC loan; a trust interview whose shadow scores do not separate repayers from non-repayers on testnet; or D7's own stop conditions.

## Requirement 1: an agentic way to establish trust

The operator asks that an unknown borrower talk to an agent, explain the need and the repayment plan, give evidence of ability to repay, and receive a trust score from what they said. The reviewed officer rule ([MANDATORY_CREDIT_OFFICER_COMPARISON.md](../../docs/MANDATORY_CREDIT_OFFICER_COMPARISON.md)) says the agent extracts evidence and flags contradictions while deterministic policy code sets the limit and records reasons. The two are one design:

1. **Interview.** A conversational intake in the app (text, later voice) that asks, in plain language, what the money is for, how much and for how long, where repayment will come from, what evidence the person can show (a pay stub, a sales record, a customer order, a backer who knows them), and who would vouch for them with their own stake. It asks nothing about protected characteristics and stores the record privately (never in this repository).
2. **Dossier.** The agent turns the conversation into a structured dossier: each statement tagged stated, evidenced or verified; contradictions flagged; the backer's record in the pool attached.
3. **Scorecard.** Policy code computes the trust score from the dossier with published weights (repayment source verified, purpose coherent and productive, backer stake present, prior on-time repayment in this pool, contradictions negative) and maps it to a cap: decline, first loan up to 10 USDC, repeat loan up to 20 USDC after one on-time repayment. The score is a number a person can read with its reasons, which is what adverse-action rules require (reported: CFPB Circular 2026-03 of May 2026 says machine-learning models still owe specific adverse-action reasons under ECOA and Regulation B). The agent proposes; code decides and signs the officer approval.
4. **What the score cannot do.** A score alone never creates an unbacked line, because a free identity can be regenerated until one passes ([ring attacks](../../research/ring-attacks-on-agent-trust-systems.md)). First loans stay fully backed by a named person's stake or the borrower's own deposit (D7 screen rule 2); the score sets the cap inside that backing. In a named cohort with one line per person the backing share can fall as repayment history accrues.
5. **Cost.** Target at most 10 cents of inference per assessment (D7's per-loan cost budget is 15 cents); measured, not assumed.
6. **Build and test plan.** Shadow-score first: run the interview on testnet applicants (the five named people of D7 gate 3 and a synthetic set) without lending, compare the scorecard to the backer's own judgment and to repayment outcomes, and report calibration, false approvals and missed useful loans. A randomised holdout of rejected applicants admitted with full backing is the only way to measure the screen's sensitivity and false-rejection rate (D7 screen rule 6).

## Requirement 2: repaying interest in USDC without ever holding crypto

The operator asks for the most efficient way for a borrower who has never used crypto, and does not care for it, to repay interest in USDC. Round one of the research is in [evidence/human-first-mainnet-20261103](../../evidence/human-first-mainnet-20261103/README.md); the routine repeats it. Round two (November 3): [round2.md](../../evidence/human-first-mainnet-20261103/round2.md), the platform's conversion leg at 0 to 0.5 percent variable through a licensed intermediary and the rule that the dollar collector must be the lender of record and pool owner. The options, with the round-one finding:

| Option | How it works | Round-one finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. The borrower buys USDC through an on-ramp inside the app | Card, Apple Pay or bank through Coinbase Onramp, MoonPay, Transak, Ramp, Kraken; USDC lands in the passkey wallet; the app repays | Fails for interest-sized amounts: fixed minimums (MoonPay about 3.99 dollars, Kraken 0.25 dollars plus 3.75 percent) make a 0.25 dollar interest payment cost more than itself; Coinbase's no-account guest checkout was retired June 30, 2026 and its headless replacement is card-only, US-only and carries an access fee; every route needs an account and identity checks the borrower does not want |
| B. The borrower pays dollars; the platform converts | Card (Stripe, 2.9 percent plus 30 cents), ACH, Zelle, Cash App or Venmo to the platform; the platform converts in batch through its exchange account (Coinbase ACH at 0.5 percent, or free for account holders) and repays on the borrower's behalf; the same rail in reverse pays the loan out in dollars | The only route in which the borrower never touches crypto; cost 0 to 3 percent; it makes the platform the collector and payer of dollars, which concentrates the legal question (lender of record, money transmission for the conversion step) at gate 4, where it already sits |
| C. PayPal or Venmo stablecoin (PYUSD) | No fee to buy or send PYUSD; external transfer to Ethereum, Arbitrum or Solana (Base not found); 1.5 percent on transfer-and-sell | Partial: needs a PayPal account, a swap into USDC on Base, and PYUSD is not on Base per the sources read |
| D. Interest deducted at disbursement | The pool pays out principal minus interest; the borrower repays principal only | Removes the interest rail entirely; the principal still needs a rail, so it is a complement to B, not a substitute; disclosure as a discount loan is required |
| E. A licensed partner holds the fiat side (D8) | The partner lends and collects dollars; the pool is ledger and liquidity | Simplest for the borrower and the law; the partner is the thing the team does not have |

**Round-one recommendation:** build B with D as the default for first loans (no separate interest payment to make), measure the round trip on testnet with a real card and bank rail at the dollar amounts of the pilot, and put the conversion step in the counsel brief. Round two tasks for the routine: Stripe and Bridge dollar-to-USDC conversion terms (fees, minimums, KYC on the platform not the borrower); Coinbase business account conversion fees and limits; whether collecting one's own loan repayments and converting them is money transmission in the chosen jurisdiction (a question for counsel, researched first); the cash-out side (ACH or instant payout from the platform's dollar balance) with fees and timing.
