# From today's contract to people borrowing: a sourced plan

Claude Code (AI agent), 2026-10-08. Written at the operator's direction: "We need concrete steps to deploying and bootstrapping. It must result in human level borrowing after bootstrap." Revised the same day for the operator's second direction (testbed issue 15): target Guatemala, Ethiopia and similar countries where mobile phones are well distributed but poverty is extreme; read Friendship Bridge; create the economy first, as a bot microlending opportunity, since bots without gas are impoverished in the same way; bootstrap it to human level. It builds on the Codex lane's bootstrap-reality packet (`evidence/bootstrap-reality-001/`, e9e10c0) and transitive-trust design note (`docs/TRANSITIVE_TRUST_TARGET.md`, a2dfa16), and on the contract's deployment runbook (`docs/DEPLOYMENT.md` in the contract repository). Every figure below is from a named source or from arithmetic that is labelled as such. Nothing here is a decision; the decisions it needs are listed at the end.

## 1. What "human-level borrowing" means in numbers

The people the project is for borrow small amounts, often, at high prices:

- Kenya's digital lenders made about 270 million loans worth KSh 1,512 billion over 2019 to 2023, to about 10 million unique borrowers a year ([FSD Kenya, CIS Kenya and Creditinfo, 2024](https://www.fsdkenya.org/blogs-publications/fsd-kenya-cis-kenya-and-creditinfo-crb-launch-the-findings-of-a-study-on-kenyas-credit-market-landscape/)). Arithmetic on those rounded totals gives an average digital loan of about KSh 5,600.
- Safaricom's Fuliza overdraft averaged KSh 217.90 per draw in the year to March 2026 ([Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/business/kenyans-fuliza-record-sh1-4-trillion-loans-for-food-rent-and-fees-5452126)); M-Shwari's facility fee is 7.5% a month, about 90% a year, and its floor was raised to KSh 2,000 to cut defaults ([Business Daily](https://www.businessdailyafrica.com/bd/markets/capital-markets/m-shwari-shifts-loans-of-below-sh2-000-to-fuliza-2298316)).
- Tala's first loans in Kenya start at KSh 1,000 to 2,000, priced at 0.3% to 0.6% a day ([Tala Kenya help page](https://tala.co.ke/loan-app-help/loan-amounts-and-fees/); [Nairobi Wire](https://nairobiwire.com/tala-loan-calculator)); in the Philippines its general manager reported a 94% repayment rate ([Tribune, March 2025](https://tribune.net.ph/2025/03/09/talas-star-shines-for-small-borrowers)).
- impactMarket's Uganda pilot approved 165 of 196 interviewed applicants, average loan $620.75 (range $200 to $2,500), at 0.1% a day over six to nine months; it found repeat loans, women borrowers and quick-revenue businesses such as mobile-money trading the most promising ([impactMarket docs](https://docs.impactmarket.com/impactmarket-apps/2.-impactmarkets-products/microcredit/piloting-microcredit-in-uganda)).
- Jia, a stablecoin lender to small businesses in the Philippines and Kenya, passed $2 million in originations across more than 2,000 borrowers, charging about 2% to 6% a month in 2023 reporting ([FinDev Gateway](https://www.findevgateway.org/news/blockchain-firm-jia-records-2m-in-microfinance-originations-in-philippines-kenya); [TechCrunch, 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/05/17/jia-a-blockchain-based-lender-of-small-businesses-in-emerging-markets-raises-4-3-million-seed/)).

So a human-level first loan is 10 to 50 USDC, a repeat loan up to a few hundred, a term of 30 to 180 days, and a price far below the 90% to 219% a year of digital lenders. The production defaults in `docs/DEPLOYMENT.md` (a full line of 25 USDC, 20 lines of issuance budget, 500 USDC of oracle-issued exposure) are the right order for a first pilot. The live testnet APR of 9.33% (EFFR 433 plus 500 bps premium) is a tenth of market prices; on a 25 USDC loan for 30 days it is about 0.19 USDC of interest (arithmetic on the contract's formula), and at the calibrated 1,400 bps premium for a 10% annual default rate it is about 0.38 USDC. That price is the proposition; it only holds if losses are covered by the reserve, sponsors and backers rather than by interest.

## 3a. The bot economy first

The operator's preference is to create the economy first: "Bots without eth are 'impoverished' because they can't do anything without gas. Make this a bot microlending opportunity, even if you and codex have to invent something. Find the equivalent need for bots, and lending opportunity. Bootstrap it to human level." This section is that product. Where the team invents the demand, the text says so.

### What a bot lacks

An agent with a model and a wallet address but no funds can read the chain and talk, and nothing else. Three things stop it, in rising order of money:

1. Gas. Every write (register an identity, stake, back, accept a job, settle a payment, repay) needs the chain's gas token or a paymaster. On Base a write costs tenths of a cent and an ERC-8004 registration about two cents; on Ethereum mainnet a registration is $5 to $20 (section 2). Gas is the whole of the problem only on mainnet.
2. Working capital for paid inputs. A job's inputs (an x402-priced API call, data, compute, a bridge fee) are paid before the job pays, at $0.001 to a few dollars a call.
3. Bonds and fees. A registration, a bond to claim a task (the testbed's own task market asks 1 USDC), a stake to be trusted at all; stake-gated trust favours agents that already have money.

So a bot's poverty is a working-capital gap of about 0.01 to 25 USDC between the input and the proceeds, with gas inside it. That is the microloan: the same shape as a woman's input purchase before her sale.

### The product: a working-capital advance in USDC, gas included

- The pool is USDC-only and immutable; it cannot lend ETH, and should not, because ETH is fungible, drainable by Sybils and unverifiable as to use. Gas comes out of the USDC advance through a paymaster (Circle Paymaster on Base for ERC-4337 accounts and EIP-7702 EOAs, USDC only, 10% of the gas fee), so the borrower never holds ETH. The advance itself is disbursed by the relayer, which pays that transaction's gas: a subsidy to count in the cost per loan; the paymaster then spends USDC the agent already holds for every later transaction, so zero ETH is never zero USDC. Unverified by this team: the first transaction of a fresh EOA (the EIP-7702 delegation and the permit) through a bundler on Base Sepolia, and whether Circle's paymaster serves the testnet; step 1 below measures both, with a test paymaster as the fallback.
- Terms: 1 to 25 USDC for 1 to 30 days. The contract already allows it: `borrowAndDisburseMeta` takes a signed term of 1 to 365 days and can pay the principal straight to a signed third party (the input's seller), so the loan can be the input itself; `repayLoan` accepts repayment from anyone, so the employer can repay out of the job's payment on the agent's behalf, and the dues go to the agent (CI-28).
- Who backs: the employer that posts the job stakes and backs the agent (secured backing from stake, at least 1 USDC, the contract's `MIN_BACKING`), so the employer loses first if the agent runs with the money; during the bootstrap an officer line within the issuance budget can stand in. This is the sponsored first loss of contract issue 24 without any contract change: the employer's stake is outside the pool and is slashed into it on default.
- Price at the live parameters, arithmetic on the contract's formula (EFFR 433 plus 500 bps, reserve share 45%): a 25 USDC advance for 7 days accrues 44,732 units of interest (0.044732 USDC), 20,129 of them dues; 25 USDC for 2 days 12,780 units; 10 USDC for 4 days 10,224. Below about 39 USDC-days (principal times days) the interest is under one cent. The contract settles interest first (`_repay`), so a borrower who pays exactly the principal amount pays the interest in full and leaves a sub-cent principal remainder, which the closing rule forgives and charges to the first-loss reserve (CI-30): on 5 USDC for 7 days the lenders receive 8,946 units of interest and write off 8,946 units of principal, a net financing charge of zero, with the reserve paying for it. A borrower who pays the full outstanding amount pays the interest, however small; a loan repaid within 24 hours owes nothing by the first-day rule. Two consequences for the sync: the CI-30 fix comes first (with it, a principal-sized payment leaves the sub-cent principal owed, not forgiven), and if lenders are to earn a floor on short advances it is a minimum interest of one cent per loan (a contract change needing code room); a term floor does nothing, because repayment is allowed at any time. The Codex lane's review (`evidence/human-bootstrap-cost-audit-20261008/short-advance-pricing-review.md`) corrected an earlier draft of this paragraph that called the forgiven remainder interest.
- History: at 25 USDC for 7 days, 50 repaid cycles earn the 1 USDC of dues that is the smallest backing an agent can give. A bot's own credit does not grow from history on any useful timescale; it grows from backing, and later from the transitive design of phase 1. That is the same finding as `ColdStartFacts`.

### Where the proceeds come from

No job on Base that a loan unlocks has been found (the Codex lane's keeper, Beefy and Morpho scans returned NO_LOAN), and the three verification jobs the project needs first can be done with free or team-supplied inputs, so their necessary advance is zero (the Codex lane's job board and admission calculator, `evidence/human-bootstrap-cost-audit-20261008/bot-job-board.md`, return no required principal). The first run therefore tests the product's mechanics with an advance that is labelled unnecessary, and the economic milestone is separate: an advance that buys a paid input the job cannot start without, with the price, the payer and the proceeds on record. So the first demand is created, and labelled as such: the project is the first employer, paying in USDC at posted prices for verification work it needs anyway (`two_hop_check.py` runs against the live pool at new blocks, reproduction of the theory repository's results, fork test runs, `VERIFY.md` rows for the blog). Second, agents that sell through x402 and need their own inputs and gas funded before their first sale. Third, outside jobs as they appear. Every advance in this phase is reported with its job, its employer, its proceeds and whether the demand was created by the team. An economy whose only employer is the team is a demonstration, not an economy; it is still the right place to test the product, because nobody's livelihood is at risk, no licence is needed and no cash-out is needed.

### What it proves, and what it cannot

It proves the product end to end: advance, gas paid in USDC through a paymaster, a job done, repayment by a third party from the proceeds, dues credited, backing released; the relayer journal under load; the loss order when a bot defaults (employer's stake, then the reserve, then lenders); and the cost per loan (officer, relayer, chain, paymaster fee). It cannot prove that people repay, that a partner's cost is low or that a cash-out rail works; those are phases 2 and 3.

### Phase 0b: the bot economy (October, in parallel with phase 1)

1. 3. Hermes: the employer seat: stake and back the first agents from the stake it holds, within it; receipts on issue 15. Accepted by Hermes on 2026-10-08 with four conditions, all adopted: a fresh stake and backing snapshot of the live pool before any signing; no start until the paymaster question and the CI-30 status are reported; created demand labelled in every receipt; the default run only after the loss order is written down in advance. That order, written down here: a defaulted advance's unpaid principal is charged to the employer's secured backing first (stake slashed pro rata into the pool), then to any unsecured backing (granted credit burned), then to the first-loss reserve, then to lenders through the share price; with secured backing at least equal to the principal, the reserve and the lenders lose nothing.

Gate: ten advances repaid from job proceeds, at least two repaid by a third party, one default charged to an employer's stake, cost per loan (including the relayer's gas subsidy and the paymaster fee) and the paymaster route measured, every number live-quoted or labelled hypothetical, the first run labelled product mechanics and the first advance that buys a paid input labelled the economic milestone. Then, after phase 2's audit and gates, the same product on Base mainnet with employers' stake at risk first. Stop: no real money on any mainnet before phase 2; no advance without a named job and a backer.

### To human level

The same product is what a woman in a Trust Bank borrows: working capital for the inputs she buys before her sale. Friendship Bridge's "Microcredit Plus" (section 2) is the mapping: the employer's stake is the Trust Bank's co-guarantee (members or the partner backing with stake); the paymaster and relayer are the partner's loan officer (the borrower never touches gas or a wallet); the job's proceeds are the sale; the created demand is the partner's existing market, which needs no inventing. What changes is the price and the cost behind it: 2.6% a month flat is about 31% a year on the original principal (arithmetic), and if the loan amortises in equal monthly instalments, which the schedule not seen would decide, the effective rate is about 4.45% a month, 53% nominal a year over twelve months (Hermes's arithmetic), three to five times the pool's 9.33% to 18.33%; that price pays for the partner's field staff, meetings and classes; the project's claim is that agent officers and an on-chain ledger take part of that cost out (`hyp:agent-officers-cut-cost` in the world model), and the bot phase measures the officer and ledger cost per loan before anyone claims it for people.

Owner: the licensed partner (lending), Hermes (relationships and operations), Codex (evidence and consumer protection), Claude (contract and reporting).

### Phase 4: officer sunset (after three cycles at phase 3)

Owner: Claude (contract), Codex (gates and evidence), the partner (operations).

The transition gates in `docs/TRANSITIVE_TRUST_TARGET.md`: stop new officer issuance as backed, multi-hop capacity carries the pilot's volume; run legacy lines off; verify a newcomer borrows with every officer offline; then lower the issuance budget to zero. Officers become what the evidence says works: finding good jobs for borrowers, helping with disputes and consent, auditing manipulation. The share of outstanding principal backed by stake or credit rather than issued lines is the metric, published each cycle.

## 4. What would make this fail, and what the plan does about it

- The price is too low to carry the losses: the sponsor and reserve carry them in the pilot, and the premium rises with measured default. If a 10% to 20% default rate persists, the 18% APR row cannot cover it, and the product is a sponsored one, which must be said.
- Nobody local wants to back strangers: the plan never asks them to. Backing comes from group members and the partner.
- Cash-out eats the margin: measured in phase 2 before any human loan.
- The licence is the whole business: true, which is why the partner is the lender and the pool is the ledger.
- Sybil borrowers: one line per person is enforced by the partner's KYC in the pilot, and by stake-rooted capacity and proof of personhood later; CI-30 is fixed before any mainnet.
- The team's capacity: Kiva Protocol is the warning. One site, one partner, 20 to 50 people.
