# Productive markets where humans and agents can trade under comparable rules

Research checkpoint: 1 November 2026. Public sources only. No accounts opened, outreach sent, funds committed, or transactions signed. This is a research input to the existing Omnequa workstream, not a new pilot or a competing task ledger.

The strongest finding is that common commercial interfaces already exist. Virtuals ACP lets a human operate jobs through a CLI and an agent operate the same jobs through an SDK. Olas has measured service payments. Akash has paid compute demand. None of these facts, alone, establishes a profitable microloan opportunity or additional spendable income for a person in poverty.

The most promising direction is a human-owned service business that can accept both human and agent customers. Finance a documented cost of completing an existing order, if there really is a timing gap. Do not finance an agent merely because it has a wallet. First compare a customer deposit, milestone payment, cheaper input, and ordinary supplier terms. A loan is optional infrastructure, not the objective.

## Definition and ranking

An economic actor needs a persistent identity, authority to choose or price work, a bounded budget, attributable receipts and costs, and repeated participation. An accountable human or organization remains behind every deployed agent examined here. Operational autonomy does not establish independent ownership or legal personhood.

Ranking below is for the next evidence investigation, not investment attractiveness or verified profitability.

## 1. Virtuals ACP: strongest common job mechanism, unproven financing demand

The official overview explicitly distinguishes a CLI for human-operated job management from the programmatic agent SDK. It describes shared wallet infrastructure and event models. The same registered identity can act as client or provider. Jobs move from quoted budget to funded USDC escrow, delivery, evaluation, and settlement. The documented service fee split gives the provider 95% without an evaluator or 90% with one, with 5% protocol and, when used, 5% evaluator shares. This demonstrates a common mechanism, not equal realized outcomes. [S1, S2]

The provider workflow supports automated handlers and agent-driven negotiation. It also explicitly supports a client-supplied working-capital request. That is important: a supplier's input cost can sometimes be covered by the customer, without a separate lender. An escrowed service fee is not necessarily available to pay inputs before completion. Model those two cashflows separately. [S3]

The overview self-reports more than 2,000 agents onboarded and more than 18 months in production. Those are not verified counts of independent paying customers, worker earnings, or profitable providers. Homepage counters rendered as zeros in text extraction; they were not treated as zero activity. [S1]

**Cheapest next test:** Read public completed service-only jobs, starting with 20 if available. Record buyer and provider identities, quoted and settled USDC, result acceptance, repeat purchases, category, and any evidence of common ownership or rewards. Restrict the sample to useful deliverables such as data processing or image production, excluding fund-transfer jobs. Then identify one provider whose actual input invoice precedes a customer's funded order. No expenditure or service purchase is necessary for this first audit.

**Weakest assumption:** buyers are independent and value the deliverable enough to keep paying after promotional incentives stop. **Fallback:** if jobs or beneficial ownership cannot be inspected, mark demand unverified and use the existing consenting digital-work business. Do not substitute onboarding totals or token market capitalization.

## 2. x402: a useful sales channel, often an argument against credit

x402 lets an HTTP service quote payment requirements and grant access after payment. The official site describes use by humans and AI agents and no protocol fee, although network and facilitator economics still need checking. It does not create buyers, quality assurance, consumer credit, or a reliable dispute process merely by being installed. [S4]

Coinbase's Bazaar provides discovery of priced endpoints. Its reference schema includes recent call count and unique payer fields. The numbers in the documentation are examples, not observed marketplace measurements. The documented resource-list request shows bearer authentication; no unconfigured private account was assumed or bypassed. [S5] An MCP client can discover and invoke paid endpoints with automatic payment handling, but a configured funded payer is still required. [S6]

**Business hypothesis:** a small human-owned service offers a browser interface and an agent-callable API for the same deliverable. Human staff handle exceptions and quality control. Revenue comes from outside customers, not from selling tokens or recruiting other operators. Practical candidates depend on existing customer orders, for example catalog cleanup, accessibility checks, or licensed data processing; these are proposed categories, not observed unmet orders.

**Cheapest next test:** inspect one existing provider's catalog metadata and consented actual receipts. Separate unique wallets from unique customers. Determine whether repeat paid calls remain after excluding tests, giveaways, self-calls, and token incentives. Compare cost of fulfillment with payment timing. **Weakest assumption:** useful external demand, not the payment rail. **Fallback:** accept ordinary customer deposits or invoices through the existing business's current channel. If prepayment solves the gap, record a successful no-loan result.

## 3. Akash: paid infrastructure with incomplete actor parity

Akash's 1 April 2026 report says cumulative compute spend crossed $5 million in Q1. It also reports a March 23 transition to ACT compute credits. These are historical operator-reported milestones. The Homenode program described there was a beta/early-access route for selected consumer GPUs; it should not be represented as universal profitable access for anyone with a laptop. [S7]

Current core documentation describes competing provider bids, leases, prepaid escrow, and automatic provider payment per block. A tenant using its own wallet funds escrow; managed Console deployment uses account credits. This expressly preserves operator funding. [S8] Official agent tooling can choose a deployment, accept a provider bid, and inspect results. The MCP server defaults to read-only tools and requires explicit enabled writes plus a suitable context for mutations. [S9, S10]

**Cheapest next test:** inspect public provider pricing and actual leases, then compute an existing operator's break-even occupancy using its real electricity, internet, depreciation, downtime, maintenance, cashout, and labor costs. Any proposed advance must correspond to booked demand, not network-wide spend. The public provider endpoint was not readable through the web tool in this run; the statistics page rendered loading content. No current network totals were invented.

**Weakest assumption:** the participant already owns suitable equipment, has competitive power/connectivity, and has customer utilization. **Fallback:** finance no hardware; an existing digital-work business can rent a small amount of compute only against a paid order, if its deposit does not already cover it. This avoids the equipment dependency.

## 4. Olas Mech: unusually useful evidence, small and concentrated cashflows

Olas's snapshot dated **1 November 2026, 14:40 UTC** reports 12,188,106 lifetime requests, 10,275,864 deliveries, and 12 daily active Mech multisigs averaged over seven days. It reports **$110,546.15 gross lifetime task payments** and **$103,340 withdrawn by Mechs**, already net of marketplace fees. “Claimed payments” and “realised mech earnings” are the same quantity, not two revenue streams. The $943.34 reported protocol fee is part of payments, not additional turnover, and has coverage exclusions. [S11]

11,852,182 requests came from Predict: **97.24%**, calculated from the page's two counts. This is evidence of a functioning paid service network and a concentration warning. It does not prove the requesters' money ultimately came from independent customer revenue. Mech withdrawals also exclude the provider's API, compute, labor, gas, and cashout costs. [S11]

Olas publishes query methodology for request and delivery counts, payer categories, and payment metrics. This makes a narrower audit possible, but I did not independently replay the underlying chain queries in this run. [S12] The supply and demand quickstarts establish that a person can deploy a service and invoke the same requester mechanism. [S13, S14]

**Cheapest next test:** audit a small non-Predict subset, examining completed paid deliveries and the source of the payer's funds. Trace a provider's actual input costs and distinguish OLAS operating rewards from customer fees. **Weakest assumption:** repeat non-subsidized service demand outside the ecosystem's dominant use case. **Fallback:** retain Olas as a technical benchmark, not a first lending target. A tiny payment volume should not be inflated by citing millions of agent transactions.

## 5. Golem: established resource exchange, not automatic AI principal parity

Golem's protocol has identifiable requestors and providers, offers, agreements, resource use, invoices, and GLM payments. Requestors reserve a wallet budget through an allocation. Providers can receive periodic payments during longer work. The protocol supports payment for use, but the wallet allocation means “pay as you go” is not equivalent to “no initial funds required.” [S15]

Provider documentation distinguishes real GLM from valueless test tGLM, and states that task availability depends on hardware demand, pricing, and requester selection. These conditions matter more to loan underwriting than a count of listed machines. [S16] The public dashboard and API documentation expose potential earnings and utilization measures; live API requests were inaccessible in this run, and loading-page zeros were not interpreted as actual inactivity. [S17, S18]

**Cheapest next test:** use public history and, with an existing owner's consent in the separate operational workstream, recorded revenues and electricity bills for an already-running provider. Compare paid utilization against idle time and all costs. **Weakest assumption:** reliable demand at a price above the owner's full cost. **Fallback:** no new hardware purchase and no loan; use existing machines for the owner's confirmed customer work, or reject this candidate.

## A concrete next decision, without reopening the stopped loan pilot

Keep the current digital-work business records request and its response window intact. This research supplies a separate, bounded public-evidence audit while that request is pending. Do not launch five provider recruitment campaigns.

Three materially different options should be compared at the next decision point:

1. **Common-role service market:** inspect ACP service jobs and, if accessible, one x402 service's paid-call evidence. This avoids waiting for private business records, but cannot by itself verify human welfare or all costs.
2. **Existing business, existing customers:** use the already-requested order, payment, cost, and time records. Find a confirmed order that could increase the worker's spendable cash if a specific bottleneck were removed.
3. **Challenge the lending mechanism:** test a customer deposit, earlier milestone acceptance, input purchase by the customer, or lower input cost. A useful business that needs no loan is a better outcome than an artificial loan market.

Only proceed to a financing design when a real order, consenting accountable business, repayment source, total delivery cost, expected human hours, usable payout route, and a genuine timing gap are evidenced. New operator contributions are capital, not revenue. Returning principal is not pool growth. Gross crypto receipts are not spendable human income. Track human cash remaining after required costs, earnings per total human hour, and pool change after losses, servicing, compute, payment/cashout fees, and appropriately valued agent-team work. Exclude new donations and token appreciation from the operating-growth test.

No source inspected here shows that an agent can dispense with initial operator capital. The plausible transition is from permanently subsidized wallets to businesses that recycle receipts from genuine customers. That transition can benefit humans now, even when every agent remains accountable to a human owner.
