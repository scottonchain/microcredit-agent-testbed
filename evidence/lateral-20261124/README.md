## Receipt (N5, nonpayment recovery through existing law): Colorado's route, read from the statute

**Test.** The second-pass set allows N5 a re-read on new primary data. The new data is the project's own state: the November 11 map read New York, Illinois and Los Angeles; the lender of record, the loans and the first jobs the guarantee would cover are Colorado-first (D16). The counterparties are the Colorado Division of Labor Standards and Statistics (the administrative route), the county courts' small claims division (the court route) and the employer who has not paid. Their terms were read from the Colorado Revised Statutes 2024 as published by the General Assembly's Office of Legislative Legal Services (Titles 8 and 13, uncertified printouts) and, after Codex's review of the first head, from the enacted House Bill 25-1001 (signed May 22, 2025; the General Assembly's bill page with its "as enacted" summary and the signed act), which amended several of the sections read. The Division's own site, the Judicial Branch's self-help pages and two statute mirrors refuse this proxy, so no agency statistics were read.

**Currency of the law.** The 2024 printout is not current law on three of the terms below. HB25-1001 takes effect ninety days after the 2025 session's adjournment (absent a referendum petition) and applies to conduct on or after that date; it (a) keeps the Division's claim ceiling at 7,500 dollars for claims filed through June 30, 2026, raises it to 13,000 dollars for claims filed July 1, 2026 through December 31, 2027, and indexes it by rule from 2028; (b) repeals the requirement that the Division determine a complaint within ninety days; (c) cuts the wait before a payout from the wage theft enforcement fund from six months to 120 days after the determination; (d) lets the director waive the automatic penalty when the employer pays all claimed wages within fourteen days of the administrative claim, except for a second failure within five years; (e) narrows an employer's fee recovery to actions found to lack substantial justification; and (f) adds misclassification fines of 5,000 to 50,000 dollars and public naming of violators. The table gives the 2024 text and the enacted change side by side; the reading uses the enacted law.

**Reading.** For a worker Colorado counts as an employee, the state already runs most of what the first-job payment guarantee would do, on paper and under the enacted law: a free administrative claim up to 13,000 dollars for claims filed from July 1, 2026 through December 31, 2027 (the window that applies to a claim filed on this receipt's date; 7,500 dollars for claims filed through June 30, 2026; indexed by rule from January 1, 2028), a fourteen-day clock on the employer, an automatic penalty of twice the wages or 1,000 dollars (waivable once if the employer pays within fourteen days of the claim), a judgment the Division itself files, public naming, and a state fund that may pay the worker 120 days after a determination if the employer has not. What the statute does not give is a date: the ninety-day determination requirement in this provision was repealed (other provisions and later amendments were not verified), the fund payout is discretionary and subject to the fund's balance, and the route depends on the worker filing. So the time from an unpaid due date to a state payout is the time to file, plus the Division's determination (no deadline remains in the provision read), plus 120 days, plus the Division's prioritization; the read gives no lower or upper bound for it, and no actual duration or payout is measured. The guarantee's remaining room for an employee is that unmeasured wait and the cases where the worker never files. For a worker found to be an independent contractor, which is a common shape of a first gig, the Division's administrative route and the fee-shifting section do not apply by their own terms; of the routes read here, small claims (31 to 55 dollars, up to 7,500 dollars, no automatic penalty, no fund) is the one left, and the new misclassification fines bear on the employer, not on the worker's recovery. This read does not inventory other remedies a contractor may have (contract claims in county or district court, local ordinances, which HB25-1001 now expressly permits). That is where N5's "notice under the applicable law before credit" pointer and the guarantee's C5 role separate: the law covers the employee strongly, on an unmeasured clock, and the contractor thinly. What the read does not establish: how many Colorado claims are filed, determined or paid from the fund, how long any step takes in practice, the fund's balance or payouts to date (the Division's pages are unreadable here), whether a referendum petition was filed against the act (none is indicated on the bill page, whose status reads "Became Law"), later 2025 or 2026 amendments, or how often a first-job worker would be classified as an employee under 8-4-101(5); a statute is the route's terms, not its use.
