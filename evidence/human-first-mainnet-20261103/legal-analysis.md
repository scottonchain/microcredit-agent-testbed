# Staying compliant without counsel: the legal analysis for a 100 USDC human-first pool (November 3, 2026)

Claude Code (AI), at the operator's direction of November 3: figure out the legal issue with online and legal research; stay compliant without involving counsel where the research allows; start from the scenario in which the team simply deploys to mainnet and contributed money cannot be withdrawn, and modify it to be as user-friendly as possible without leaving compliance. This is research, not legal advice. Primary texts were read on November 3 through the session proxy (curl) after the operator opened the hosts: 31 CFR 1010.100 and 12 CFR 1002.9, 1026.2 and 1026.3 (Legal Information Institute mirror of the eCFR), FinCEN's guidance FIN-2019-G001 (fincen.gov PDF), and the Colorado Revised Statutes Title 5 (olls.info, 2026 compilation). Two hosts still refuse the session (banking.colorado.gov and sec.gov return 403), so those two items keep their secondary label. Labels: **[primary]** quoted from the text itself; **[secondary]** a law-firm, press or industry summary.

## 1. Scenario 0: deploy, donate, walk away

The starting point the operator described: the fixed contract is deployed on Base mainnet; 100 USDC is sent in and the contract has no withdrawal path for the donor; the agent officer runs; anyone with the app can apply, borrow USDC, send it, repay USDC with interest; the operator publicises it. What the law sees in that picture:

| Element of scenario 0 | What it is in law | Exposure | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The pool lends its own reserve and takes repayment | Lending, not money transmission. Money transmission is "the acceptance of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency from one person and the transmission of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency to another location or person" [primary: 31 CFR 1010.100(ff)(5)(i)(A)]. A lender is the counterparty, not a conduit, and the regulation also excludes a person that "accepts and transmits funds only integral to the sale of goods or the provision of services, other than money transmission services, by the person who is accepting and transmitting the funds" [primary: (ff)(5)(ii)(F)]. | None from the lending itself. **The operator's intuition is right here, and neither the donation nor the autonomy is what makes it right.** | High |
| An autonomous contract with no identifiable operator | FinCEN: "when DApps perform money transmission, the definition of money transmitter will apply to the DApp, the owners/operators of the DApp, or both"; "the developer of a DApp is not a money transmitter for the mere act of creating the application … However, if the developer of the DApp uses or deploys it to engage in money transmission, then the developer will qualify" [primary: FIN-2019-G001 sections 4.4 and 5.2.2]. The CFTC's 2023 default judgment treated Ooki DAO's governing token holders as an unincorporated association that is a "person" liable for the protocol's unregistered activity [secondary]. The Tornado Cash prosecution reached a developer whose non-custodial contract moved other people's value (August 2025 conviction on the unlicensed-transmission conspiracy count; acquittal motion later granted; retrial of the hung counts set for April 2027) [secondary, reports conflict]. The CLARITY Act, which would have codified that non-controlling developers are not transmitters, failed Senate cloture 50 to 49 on September 15, 2026 [secondary]. | Autonomy does not make the pool nobody's. Whoever deploys, funds, parameterises, runs the officer and publicises it is the person the law looks for. Since the pool does not transmit, that person is not a transmitter; but they are the **lender**, and lending law attaches to them. | High that the operator is the lender in substance; the DAO and developer cases show how regulators reach controllers |
| Borrowers are strangers anywhere in the world | A person "regularly engaged in the business of making loans" to individuals for personal, family or household purposes is making consumer loans [primary: C.R.S. 5-1-301(15)], and every state has its own version, many with licences for any consumer loan with interest [secondary]. Outside the United States, each country's rules. | Unbounded: fifty states' rules plus foreign law, with no way to know which applies to a given borrower. | High |
| Interest at the live 9.33 percent a year | Below Colorado's 12 percent line for unsupervised consumer loans [primary: 5-2-201(1)] and below every state cap found (17 to 36 percent at the low end) [secondary]. | None on rate, in Colorado. | High for Colorado |
| The borrower must obtain USDC to repay and must convert USDC to spend the loan | The borrower buys and sells CVC through exchanges, each of which is a licensed exchanger. If the operator steps in to convert for the borrower, the operator is "a person engaged as a business in the exchange of virtual currency for real currency" [primary: FIN-2019-G001 section 4, quoting the 2013 guidance], a money transmitter. | None for the operator in scenario 0 as such; but the user experience is the register's cash-out problem, and the obvious "fix" (convert for them) is exactly what creates the transmitter exposure. | High |
| The officer scores applicants with a model | ECOA applies to every creditor and to business credit; a creditor must give "specific" reasons for adverse action, and "statements that the adverse action was based on the creditor's internal standards or policies or that the applicant … failed to achieve a qualifying score on the creditor's credit scoring system are insufficient" [primary: 12 CFR 1002.9(b)(2)]. | A score with no reasons is a violation on the first decline. | High |
| Interview records of applicants | A non-bank lender is a financial institution for the Gramm-Leach-Bliley privacy and safeguards rules [secondary, general]. | Privacy notice and safeguards owed. | Medium |
| Anyone's wallet can receive | OFAC sanctions apply to everyone. | A disbursement to a sanctioned address is a violation. | High |
| Backers stake behind strangers and earn dues | Commissioner Peirce's statement of July 22, 2026: vaults and lending strategies may be investment contracts when returns depend on a manager's decisions; on-chain loans may be "notes" [secondary; the SEC and CFTC interpretation of March 17, 2026 excludes protocol staking, not lending, from securities offerings]. | A yield to third parties chosen by an officer looks like the described case. | Medium |
| The donor cannot withdraw | No investor, no expectation of profit, no capital solicitation: the cleanest fact in the design for the securities analysis. No charitable deduction without a qualified recipient [secondary, general]. A bug or a legal order cannot be answered. | Helpful on securities; harmful on operations. | High |

**Reading scenario 0 honestly:** it is already safe on the point the operator asked about (money transmission) and already unsafe on three others (an unnamed lender with unbounded jurisdictions, a scoring model without reasons, backers paid for strangers' risk), and it is unfriendly to the people it is for (they must buy and sell crypto to use it).

## 2. Scenario 1: the same pool, made user-friendly without leaving compliance

Each step below changes one thing in scenario 0. The order is the order of importance; every step keeps the previous ones.

| Step | Change | Why it is compliant | What the borrower notices |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Name the lender of record | The operator lends as a sole proprietor, or forms an LLC or nonprofit corporation that owns the deployer key, the officer key and the bank and exchange accounts. The loan agreement, the notices, the tax return and complaints point at that person. | Replaces an argument nobody has won ("the contract is the lender") with the structure every statute expects. Costs nothing as a sole proprietor; an LLC is a filing. | Nothing; the app can still read "Omnequa". |
| 2. Colorado first | Lend only to Colorado residents until a per-state row exists for another state. The app asks for state of residence and declines others politely. | One state's law governs, and it is the home state. The analysis below is Colorado's; other states are a row each (does it license business-purpose lenders, does it have a commercial-financing disclosure law and what is its volume floor, what is its consumer cap). | A residence question at intake. |
| 3. Business-purpose loans first (Door A) | The interview records what the money is for; loans are for an order, stock, a tool, a licence, a deposit on work: a productive input. The agreement states the purpose. | Colorado's UCCC says "consumer loan" does not include "a loan for a business, investment, or commercial purpose" [primary: 5-1-301(15)(b)(I)], so the UCCC's licence, rate ceiling and remedies do not reach these loans at all. Regulation Z exempts "an extension of credit primarily for a business, commercial or agricultural purpose" [primary: 12 CFR 1026.3(a)(1)]. Most states do not license business-purpose lending [secondary]; Colorado has no commercial-financing disclosure law [secondary]. | The interview asks what the loan is for, which the design wanted anyway. |
| 4. Consumer-purpose loans at or below 12 percent, and no more than 25 a year (Door B) | For a Colorado resident whose purpose is genuinely household, lend at the live rate, never above 12 percent all-in, and count. | Colorado: a consumer loan "other than a supervised loan" may carry a finance charge up to "twelve percent per year on the unpaid balance" [primary: 5-2-201(1)]; a supervised loan is one whose finance charge "exceeds twelve percent per year" [primary: 5-1-301(47)]; only supervised loans need the licence [primary: 5-2-301]. The UCCC's notification filing does not apply to lenders: "This part 2 does not apply to supervised lenders … [or] persons making consumer loans" [primary: 5-6-201(2)]; it is for sellers and lessors. And "regularly" in the UCCC "has the same meaning as stated in the federal Truth in Lending Act" [primary: 5-1-301(37)], which is more than 25 extensions of consumer credit in the preceding calendar year [primary: 12 CFR 1026.2(a)(17)(v)], so a lender under that count is arguably not "regularly engaged" and Regulation Z's disclosures are not owed. The pilot's 20 loans fit. | A plain statement of the rate in dollars; nothing else changes. Keep the UCCC's disclosure and remedy rules anyway as the safe course; they are short. |
| 5. Dollars in and out, the lender's own | The lender disburses in dollars from its own bank account (ACH, instant transfer) and collects repayment in dollars to its own account (ACH, Zelle, Cash App, Venmo, a card link). It records both in the pool contract and tops up the pool's USDC from its own treasury in batch, buying for its own account at a licensed exchange. First loans deduct interest at disbursement so there is no separate interest payment to make. | The lender never exchanges virtual currency for anyone else, so it is not an exchanger; the only money it moves is its own, integral to its lending [primary: (ff)(5)(ii)(F) and FIN-2019-G001 section 4]. If the team prefers not to touch dollars, a licensed provider (Stripe with Bridge, or an exchange's commerce product) collects and settles USDC to the lender; the provider is the money services business. | **The borrower never sees crypto.** They apply, get dollars, pay dollars. This is the whole user-friendliness gain, and it comes from moving the conversion to the lender's side, not from removing it. |
| 6. The interview feeds a scorecard that gives reasons | The agent extracts; deterministic code scores with published weights and emits the principal reasons; no question about race, colour, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age (beyond capacity to contract) or public-assistance income, and no proxy input (name, address, language). A notice template exists before the first decline. For business applicants with revenues of one million dollars or less the notice may be oral or written and the right to reasons may be disclosed at application [primary: 12 CFR 1002.9(a)(3)(i)]. | Satisfies Regulation B's "specific" reasons rule [primary: 1002.9(b)(2)] by construction; the CFPB's May 2026 circular on complex models (reported) is met the same way. | A short written reason when declined, which is also the fair thing. |
| 7. One person, one line; a backer or a deposit behind every first loan | As D7 already requires. | Not a legal requirement; the defence against free identities that makes the scorecard meaningful ([ring attacks](../../research/ring-attacks-on-agent-trust-systems.md)). | A vouch from someone who knows them, or a small deposit. |
| 8. Backers earn no yield; the reserve is donated; nobody is invited to deposit | Dues from interest stay in the pool; backers are people vouching for someone they name. Design Question 2 (a backer share of interest) stays closed for the pilot. | Removes the manager-dependent-return pattern Peirce described [secondary]; Kiva's US model (lenders earn nothing) is the working precedent [secondary]. | Nothing for borrowers; backers are told plainly they can lose their stake and earn nothing but the relationship. |
| 9. Privacy and sanctions | A one-page privacy notice; interview records encrypted and access-logged, never in a public repository; a short safeguards program; an OFAC name and address screen before every disbursement and a wallet screen before any on-chain payout. | GLBA and OFAC. | A privacy notice at intake. |
| 10. Keep a pause and migration key with the lender of record | The donation is irrevocable to the donor; the lender of record can pause and migrate. | Operations and legal orders need a hand on the switch; control is already accepted in step 1. | Nothing. |

**What scenario 1 gives up, and what it keeps.** It gives up the claim that nobody is the lender (which was never going to hold) and it gives up global reach on day one (fifty-plus legal systems were never going to be checked). It keeps the autonomous contract as the ledger and reserve, the donated 100 USDC that stays in the pool, the agent interview, the officer, the stakers, and the live rate. For the borrower it is strictly better than scenario 0: dollars both ways, a reason when declined, a purpose they already had.

## 3. Money transmission, in full

Federal: the definition and the "integral" exclusion are quoted above [primary]. FinCEN reads the exclusions strictly: "the person's business must be different from money transmission itself, and the money transmission activity must be necessary for the business to operate" [primary: FIN-2019-G001 section 4.5.1 discussion of FIN-2008-R007]. Lending is different from money transmission, and collecting a loan is necessary to lending. The exchanger risk is quoted above; it is avoided by converting only the lender's own money. A trading platform that "purchases the CVC from the seller and sells it to the buyer … is acting as a CVC exchanger" [primary: section 5.1]; a lender that buys USDC for its own treasury and sells none is not.

Colorado: the Division of Banking's interim guidance of December 18, 2025 says virtual currency is not within the Money Transmission Modernization Act's definition of "money", so receiving virtual currency for transmission from a person in Colorado does not need a Colorado licence, and the licence question returns when virtual currency is converted into fiat [secondary; the host returns 403 to this session; commentators disagree on the width of the gap]. A lender that does not transmit does not need the answer.

## 4. The Colorado statute, as read

| Provision | Text, as read November 3 [primary] | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5-1-301(15)(a) | "'consumer loan' means a loan made or arranged by a person regularly engaged in the business of making loans in which: (I) The consumer is a person other than an organization; (II) The debt is incurred primarily for a personal, family, or household purpose; (III) Either the debt is by written agreement payable in installments or a finance charge is made; and (IV) Either the principal does not exceed seventy-five thousand dollars or the debt is secured by an interest in land." | Four elements, all needed. |
| 5-1-301(15)(b) | "'consumer loan' does not include: (I) A loan for a business, investment, or commercial purpose; (II) A loan primarily for an agricultural purpose …" | Door A. |
| 5-1-301(37) | "'Regularly' has the same meaning as stated in the federal 'Truth in Lending Act' and the federal 'Consumer Leasing Act'." | The 25-a-year test, imported. |
| 5-1-301(47) | "'Supervised loan' means a consumer loan … in which the rate of the finance charge exceeds twelve percent per year as determined according to … section 5-2-201." | The licence line. |
| 5-2-201(1) | "With respect to a consumer loan other than a supervised loan … a lender may contract for and receive a finance charge calculated according to the actuarial method not exceeding twelve percent per year on the unpaid balance of the amount financed." | The unlicensed ceiling; the live 9.33 percent fits. Supervised loans may charge up to 36, 21 and 15 percent by tier or 21 percent flat [5-2-201(2)]. |
| 5-2-301(1) | "Unless a person is a supervised financial organization or has first obtained a license from the administrator authorizing the person to make supervised loans, the person shall not engage in the business of: (a) Making supervised loans …" | Licence only above 12 percent. |
| 5-6-201(2) | "This part 2 [notification and fees] does not apply to supervised lenders described in section 5-1-301 (46), persons making consumer loans described in section 5-1-301 (15), or to persons licensed as collection agencies …" | **No notification filing for a lender.** The earlier draft of this note said otherwise from a secondary summary; the statute corrects it. |
| 5-1-301(25) | "'Loan' includes … (I) The creation of debt by the lender's payment of or agreement to pay money to the consumer or to a third party for the account of the consumer …" | The one open question: whether a loan paid in USDC is a payment of "money". Scenario 1 pays in dollars, so the question does not arise; and Door A does not depend on it. |

## 6. The constraints, in one list

1. A named lender of record (operator queue row; default: the operator as sole proprietor in Colorado).
2. Colorado residents only until a per-state row exists.
3. Business-purpose loans (Door A) as the product; consumer-purpose only at or below 12 percent all-in and under 25 a year (Door B); no notification filing is owed for either.
4. Dollars disbursed and collected by the lender from its own accounts; conversion only of the lender's own treasury through a licensed exchange; never a conversion for a borrower; or a licensed provider as collector.
5. Donated reserve; no yield to backers; no public invitation to deposit.
6. Scorecard reasons as Regulation B reasons; no prohibited-basis or proxy inputs; notice template before the first decline.
7. Privacy notice, safeguards page, encrypted interview records, OFAC screen.
8. Pause and migration key with the lender of record.
9. One person, one line; a backer or deposit behind every first loan (D7).
10. Counsel optional: a one-hour review of the agreement and the Colorado reading is cheap insurance and does not gate any work. The mainnet packet carries this note.
