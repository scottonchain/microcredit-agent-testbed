## Compliance design this round builds on

The maintainer session's [legal analysis](legal-analysis.md) of the same day (`decision:d16-compliance-design-20261103`, PR 418, merged into this branch) settles the frame: lending is not money transmission; the only transmission risk is converting for others; so **the lender disburses and collects dollars from its own accounts and converts only its own treasury through a licensed exchange**, under a named lender of record (default: the operator as sole proprietor in Colorado), with two licence-free doors (business-purpose loans; Colorado consumer loans at or below 12 percent after the 200-dollar UCCC notification), Regulation B reasons from the scorecard, no yield to backers, and under 25 consumer loans a year. Section 4 below reaches the same rule from the FinCEN rulings and is the rails-side input to it; the earlier phrase "the platform converts for the borrower" is retired. Routes in which a third party converts the borrower's own dollars (Bridge with the borrower as customer, section 2 option (ii); stack D in section 6) are therefore not candidates but recorded fallbacks, and the cost tables price the lender's own treasury conversion only.

## What this round answers

The round-one finding stands: the crypto-naive borrower pays and receives dollars, and the lender converts its own treasury. Round two asks what the platform's conversion leg costs, who is the regulated party on it, and whether collecting one's own loan repayments in dollars and converting them is money transmission. Short answers: the conversion leg exists at 0 to 0.5 percent variable cost through a licensed intermediary (Coinbase business account or Stripe's Bridge), plus fixed monthly items; Stripe's own stablecoin products do not do this leg for a US platform; and the federal rulings separate a lender collecting its own receivable (not transmission) from a platform forwarding a borrower's dollars to someone else (transmission), which fixes a design rule for the pool.

**Two ways to use it, and which fits D16.** (i) Platform as the customer: the lender of record completes Bridge's business KYB once, holds one virtual account (its own dollars in, converted to USDC at the pool's address) and one liquidation address (pool USDC out to its own bank). Borrowers never meet Bridge; they pay the platform by whatever dollar rail they have. Variable cost 0.25 to 0.50 percent per leg, plus 2 dollars a month for the virtual account, 0.25 dollars a month per wallet, 10 dollars a year of compliance fees for the one business entity, plus gas. (ii) Borrower as the customer: each borrower completes Bridge's KYC (database tier), gets a virtual account whose destination is the pool or their passkey wallet, and pays dollars straight into it. Bridge is then the transmitter for the borrower's funds and the platform never touches them, which is the cleanest legal shape; but it puts an identity check, a 2 dollar monthly account charge, a 0.25 dollar monthly wallet charge and a 2 dollar yearly compliance fee on each borrower, which together are more than eight times the 0.25 dollar interest payment and exactly what the operator's requirement asked to avoid. Round two keeps (i) as the design and records (ii) as the fallback if counsel finds (i) is transmission.

## 4. Is collecting one's own loan repayments in dollars, and converting them, money transmission?

This is the input to the November 10 counsel brief. It is research, not advice; every ruling cited binds only its requester.

**Federal (FinCEN).** The regulation defines money transmission services as the acceptance of currency, funds or other value that substitutes for currency from one person and the transmission to another location or person by any means (31 CFR 1010.100(ff)(5)(i)(A)); convertible virtual currency is value that substitutes for currency (2013 guidance, consolidated in FIN-2019-G001 of May 9, 2019). The exclusions in (ff)(5)(ii) include the payment-processor exclusion, whose four conditions include operating through a clearance and settlement system that admits only BSA-regulated financial institutions; FinCEN says CVC payment processors generally cannot meet that condition and must register as money services businesses. A person who obtains CVC and uses it to pay for goods or services on their own behalf is a user, not a transmitter (2019 guidance). The administrative rulings on lending point both ways:

| Ruling | Facts | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FIN-2008-R011 (December 11, 2008) | A microfinance clearinghouse: lenders send funds to the company, which forwards them to its microfinance partners for borrowers | Not a money transmitter: the transmission is an integral part of the loan clearinghouse services |
| FIN-2014-R005 (April 29, 2014) | An internet escrow-style service between a buyer and a seller of goods or services: the buyer pays the company, which releases the funds to the seller when the conditions are met (not a lending ruling; it cites FIN-2008-R011 for the microfinance case) | Not a money transmitter: the transmission is a necessary and integral part of the service itself |
| FIN-2008-R009 (September 18, 2008) | A loan-acceleration product: the company debits the consumer's account and forwards the money to the consumer's creditor | Money transmitter: not integral to any transaction other than the transmission itself |
| FIN-2013-R002 (November 13, 2013) | Payable-through drafts funded with the customer's own funds or with credit the company extends to the customer | Money transmitter: not integral to the execution and settlement of any transaction other than the funds transmission itself |

**Reading for the pool, in three legs.** Leg 1: the borrower pays dollars to the lender to satisfy the borrower's own debt. The lender accepts funds as payee and transmits them to no one; that is why creditors are not money services businesses, and no exemption is needed. Leg 2: the lender converts its own dollars to USDC through a licensed exchanger (Coinbase or Bridge) and deposits them into a pool it owns. That is a purchase of CVC on its own account; the exchanger is the regulated party. Leg 3 is the trap: if the dollars are the borrower's and the platform moves them to someone else, such as backers receiving an interest share, a sponsor, or a pool whose funds belong to someone other than the collector, FIN-2008-R009 and FIN-2013-R002 describe that as transmission. Disbursement runs the same way: a lender paying out its own loan is not forwarding another person's money, and FIN-2008-R011 excused even a lender-to-borrower forwarder when integral to the service; FIN-2014-R005 applies the same integral-part reading to an escrow between a buyer and a seller, not to lending.

**Design rule that follows (for D7's packet and the contract wiring).** The entity that collects dollars is the lender of record and the owner of the pool's capital; backers are paid on chain by the contract from the lender's own interest income, never by the platform forwarding a borrower's dollars; a borrower's dollar repayment is recorded as the lender's receipt and the on-chain repayment is made from the lender's own USDC. Sponsor capital (the operator's 100 USDC) is the lender's capital, not a third party's funds held for them. If the pool's legal owner and the dollar collector are different persons, the collector is a forwarder and the analysis changes.

**State law.** The CSBS Money Transmission Modernization Act has been enacted in full or in part by 35 states as of August 2026 (CSBS; licensees in those states carry about 99 percent of reported activity); Louisiana's version took effect July 1, 2026 and Oklahoma's takes effect November 1, 2026. The model act exempts an agent of the payee (a person who collects and processes payments from a payor to a payee on the payee's behalf under a written contract); about half the states recognised that exemption at an older count; California has it at Financial Code 2010(l) with DFPI opinion letters narrowing it; Texas, Massachusetts and New York have one; Florida and the District of Columbia do not; some states treat the conduct as outside the definition altogether. The exemption matters only when the collector is not the payee. A lender collecting its own receivable is the payee. The conversion step is where state virtual-currency regimes bite: California's Digital Financial Assets Law licensing became operative July 1, 2026 for digital financial asset business activity with California residents, with penalties quoted at up to 100,000 dollars a day, and sources disagree on whether its stablecoin chapter was repealed by SB 97 on June 30, 2026; whether a lender exchanging USDC on its own account through a licensed exchanger is in scope was not found in the sources read. New York's BitLicense and the state lending licences (which are separate from money transmission) were not reached this round and go to the brief as open questions, with the round-one item on Kiva's zero-interest nonprofit model and nonprofit-lender exemptions.
